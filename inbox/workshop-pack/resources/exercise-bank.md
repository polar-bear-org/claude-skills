# Exercise Bank

The format library of the Workshop Pack. agenda-designer picks from here, run-sheet-builder builds worksheets from the run lists, and workshop-retro writes what each session taught back under the exercise it taught it about. Timings assume 8-12 people unless stated; adjust up for bigger rooms.

Every entry follows the same shape: when to use it, group and time, materials, the run list, and the classic failure mode.

---

## Openers

### Check-in round
**When:** every workshop, first 10 minutes. The point is that every voice is heard once before the work starts; people who spoke early speak again.
**Group and time:** whole room, 1 minute per person.
**Materials:** none.
**Run it:**
1. One question, related to the day but light ("what would make today worth it for you?", "what's one word for how you arrive?").
2. Go around in order, no popcorn, facilitator answers last.
3. No responses or discussion, just listening; say that rule up front.
**Failure mode:** a question too deep for minute one ("what's your biggest fear about this project") stalls the room. Keep it answerable in one breath.

### No, but / Yes, and
**When:** warming up right before any ideation or vision discussion; teaches the room how building beats blocking, in ten minutes, through their own experience.
**Group and time:** groups of 4-6, 10-12 minutes.
**Materials:** none.
**Run it:**
1. Set the scene: you're on the way to a family birthday (grandma's) with no present; the group must land a gift idea.
2. Round one: someone proposes a gift, each member responds "no, but..." and replaces it with their own idea. 3 minutes.
3. Round two: new gift proposal, each member responds "yes, and..." building on the previous idea. 3 minutes.
4. Compare the two rounds as a group: what happened to the ideas, and to the energy? Draw the conclusion together, don't lecture it.
**Failure mode:** skipping the comparison in step 4; without it this is two silly rounds instead of the room's own proof of how it should talk for the rest of the day.

### Cooking class
**When:** warming up before mapping how anything currently works (an AS-IS service blueprint, a process walk); shows that even the simplest shared activity hides different approaches worth surfacing.
**Group and time:** groups of 4-6, 12-15 minutes.
**Materials:** none.
**Run it:**
1. One participant names their favorite simple dish (scrambled eggs works every time).
2. Everyone silently thinks through their own step-by-step recipe for that dish, 2 minutes.
3. Take turns sharing the approach.
4. Land the point as it becomes evident: every person cooks even scrambled eggs their own way, and if that's true for eggs, it's true for the process we're about to map; disagreement on "how it works today" is normal and useful.
**Failure mode:** letting the sharing become a debate about the right recipe. There is no right recipe; that IS the point.

### Hopes and fears
**When:** kickoffs and sessions with tension in the room; surfaces expectations before they become resistance.
**Group and time:** whole room, 15 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its, two wall zones.
**Run it:**
1. Everyone writes 2 hopes and 2 fears for the project or session, one per sticky, 4 minutes, silent.
2. Everyone posts and reads their own aloud, one sentence each.
3. Facilitator clusters live, names the clusters back to the room, and says which fears today's session will address and which are parked where everyone can see.
**Failure mode:** collecting fears and never mentioning them again; that's worse than not asking. The close must return to the wall.

## Framing and alignment

### Context gallery
**When:** the room needs shared context (research, data, prior decisions) without an hour of presentations.
**Group and time:** whole room walking solo or in pairs, 20-30 minutes.
**Materials:** 4-6 printed one-pagers or wall posters, each holding one chunk of context; pens.
**Run it:**
1. Hang the material as stations around the room.
2. Everyone walks the gallery with a task: mark what surprises you, note one question per station, sticky directly on the posters.
3. Debrief per station: the poster owner answers the top questions in 2 minutes each, no re-presenting.
**Failure mode:** posters that are dense slides. Each station carries one idea readable in 90 seconds, or the gallery becomes silent reading of a deck.

### Assumption surfacing
**When:** before ideating or deciding on anything built on beliefs nobody has checked.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 25 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its, a wall split into "know" and "believe".
**Run it:**
1. Groups list everything the plan assumes to be true, one per sticky, 8 minutes.
2. Groups sort their own stickies: verified knowledge left, belief right.
3. Whole room: dot-vote the three riskiest beliefs (the ones that kill the plan if wrong).
4. For each of the three: what's the cheapest way to test it, captured as an action with an owner.
**Failure mode:** stopping at the wall of assumptions with no test actions; the exercise's value is step 4, protect its time.

### How Might We framing
**When:** turning research findings or pain points into ideation-ready questions; the bridge into any ideation block.
**Group and time:** pairs, then whole room, 20 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its, the pain points or insights visible on the wall.
**Run it:**
1. Explain the format: "How might we [verb] for [who] so that [outcome]". A good problem statement is one short question with enough constraints to be manageable, actionable but with no solution baked in, about the people we're helping rather than technology, revenue, or product specs, and free of technical requirements that would close off unexpected directions.
2. Pairs write 3-5 HMWs from the material on the wall, 8 minutes.
3. Post all, cluster duplicates, dot-vote the 2-3 the room will ideate on.
**Failure mode:** HMWs with the solution baked in ("how might we build an app that..."). Facilitator rewrites those live with the room, it's a teaching moment, not a correction.

## Ideation

### Crazy 8s
**When:** fast individual divergence on a chosen HMW; kills the blank page and the loudest-voice problem at once.
**Group and time:** solo work in any size room, 15 minutes total.
**Materials:** one A4 per person folded into 8 panels, markers thick enough to prevent detail.
**Run it:**
1. One HMW visible to all. Everyone folds their sheet into 8.
2. 8 sketches in 8 minutes, one per panel, 60 seconds each, facilitator calls the minute. Words allowed, art skills irrelevant, say so.
3. Everyone circles their own strongest 1-2 panels.
4. Share-out in pairs or small groups (2 minutes each), then the circled ideas go to the wall for clustering or voting.
**Failure mode:** letting people "finish this one first" past the minute mark. The time pressure IS the exercise; a gentle but absolute countdown keeps it honest.

### Brainwriting 6-3-5
**When:** the room has strong hierarchies or quiet thinkers; generates a large volume of ideas with zero speaking.
**Group and time:** tables of 6, 30 minutes; works with 4-5 per table (adjust rounds).
**Materials:** one 6-3-5 grid sheet per person (6 rows, 3 columns).
**Run it:**
1. The HMW visible. Each person writes 3 ideas in row one, 5 minutes, silent.
2. Pass the sheet left. Read the ideas above, then add 3 more in the next row: build on them, twist them, or add new, 5 minutes.
3. Repeat until sheets are full or 4-5 rounds are done (theoretical max: 108 ideas per table of 6).
4. Each person marks the strongest idea on the sheet in front of them; marked ideas go to the wall.
**Failure mode:** treating it as 6 rounds of solo brainstorming. The build instruction in step 2 is the engine; repeat it every round.

### SCAMPER
**When:** improving or evolving something that already exists (a service, a process, an offer) rather than inventing from zero.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 35 minutes.
**Materials:** the thing being evolved described on one poster; SCAMPER prompt cards or a worksheet with the seven lenses.
**Run it:**
1. Walk the seven lenses once, each as a question about the thing itself, 30 seconds each: what can be Substituted? Combined with something else? Adapted to it? Modified or Magnified? Put to other use? Eliminated? Reversed or Rearranged?
2. Each group takes 2-3 lenses (assigned, not chosen, so the awkward ones get done) and generates ideas per lens, 15 minutes.
3. Rotate: groups pass their output and add to another group's lenses, 8 minutes.
4. Each group presents its best idea per lens, straight to the wall.
**Failure mode:** the room stalls on Eliminate and Reverse because they feel destructive. Those two produce the most interesting ideas; the facilitator models one absurd example to give permission.

### Worst possible idea
**When:** the room is stiff, senior, or scared of being wrong; also a reliable post-lunch resurrection.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 20 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its.
**Run it:**
1. Reverse the HMW: "how do we make this as bad as possible for the user?" Groups generate terrible ideas, 7 minutes; encourage genuine awfulness, laughter is the signal it's working.
2. Each group picks its 3 worst.
3. Flip: for each, ask "what's the inverse, and what does this terrible idea reveal that we secretly do today?" 8 minutes.
4. The flipped insights go to the wall as real ideas.
**Failure mode:** skipping the flip because the fun part ran long. The flip is the deliverable; timebox the fun hard.

### Analogy storming
**When:** the room is stuck in industry patterns and every idea looks like the competition.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 30 minutes.
**Materials:** prepared analogy cards ("how would a luxury hotel do this? an airline? a video game? a street market?"), post-its.
**Run it:**
1. Each group draws 2 analogy cards.
2. Per card: how does that world solve our HMW? Steal mechanisms, not surfaces, 10 minutes per card.
3. Translate: which stolen mechanism survives contact with our reality? One translated idea per card to the wall.
**Failure mode:** analogies too close to home ("how would another agency do it") produce nothing. The cards should be prepared in advance and deliberately far away.

### Assumption reversal
**When:** no ideas are coming and the room needs its conventional thinking destabilized; the deeper cousin of worst possible idea.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 30-45 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its, pens, paper.
**Run it:**
1. State the challenge, visible to all.
2. Groups list the fundamental assumptions about the subject, one per sticky, 7 minutes (for a restaurant: has a menu, charges for food, serves food).
3. Reverse each assumption: write its opposite (no menu of any kind, food is free, serves no food).
4. The real work: for each reversal, ask "how could this actually be accomplished as a viable thing?" and capture every useful idea it provokes (no menu → the chef cooks from today's market for each guest; free food → pay for time, not food). 15 minutes.
5. The provoked ideas, not the reversals, go to the wall.
**Failure mode:** stopping at the reversals because they're fun to write. The reversal is the spark; step 4 is the exercise.

### Mash-ups
**When:** you know the exact quality you want to design in (speed, warmth, glamour, trust) and need ideation focused precisely on it.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 30-45 minutes.
**Materials:** post-its, pens.
**Run it:**
1. Isolate the quality you're designing for, the hardest step; one quality per sticky on the wall.
2. Brainstorm real-world businesses and brands that embody that quality, 5 minutes.
3. Layer the brand onto the challenge as a mash-up question: "what's the farmer's market version of a cafeteria?", "what's the Facebook version of a savings account?"
4. Brainstorm what that mash-up would look like in your actual context, everything on stickies to the wall, 15 minutes.
**Failure mode:** rushing step 1. A vague quality ("make it better") produces vague mash-ups; the exercise is only as sharp as the quality it isolates.

### Idea box
**When:** you need a large volume of thought-provoking combinations systematically, and the room has the patience for a structured deep-dive.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, 60-120 minutes; this is a half-block, not a filler.
**Materials:** a large grid per group (parameters as columns, variations as rows), post-its, pens.
**Run it:**
1. Select the challenge's parameters, 4-5 of them (for a physical product: material, shape, finish, placement). The test for each: would the challenge still exist without this parameter? If yes, it's not a parameter.
2. List variations under each parameter, aim for 5 per column, 15 minutes.
3. Run combinations: pick one variation per column, at random at first, and ask what this combination would be (net material + cylindrical + on the door = a basketball-hoop laundry hamper). Log every combination that provokes a real idea.
4. Narrow to the promising region of the box and mine it deliberately.
**Failure mode:** boxes too big to explore (10 parameters × 10 variations is 10 billion combinations); keep it 4-5 columns, and treat random runs as the method, not cheating.

### Round robin building
**When:** deepening a shortlist of ideas after divergence; turns fragile one-liners into fleshed concepts.
**Group and time:** groups of 3-4, one shortlisted idea per group, 25 minutes.
**Materials:** one concept sheet per idea (idea, who it serves, how it works, what makes it hard, first step to test it).
**Run it:**
1. Each group fills the concept sheet for its idea, 10 minutes.
2. Pass the sheet to the next group, whose job is to strengthen it: answer its weak spots, add what's missing, 7 minutes. No trashing allowed; improve or ask questions.
3. Return to the original group for a final pass, 5 minutes.
**Failure mode:** step 2 becoming critique. The instruction is "make this idea stronger than the group that loves it could"; police the framing, not the enthusiasm.

## Convergence and prioritization

### Affinity clustering
**When:** any wall of stickies that needs to become themes; the standard move after every divergence block.
**Group and time:** whole room at the wall, 15-20 minutes.
**Materials:** the wall of stickies, marker for cluster names.
**Run it:**
1. Silent clustering first: everyone moves stickies toward stickies they belong with, no talking, 5 minutes (silence prevents the loudest sorter deciding the taxonomy).
2. Talking pass: resolve the contested stickies, split clusters that grew into junk drawers.
3. Name each cluster in the room's words, written above it. A cluster that can't be named in five words is two clusters.
**Failure mode:** clusters named by category ("marketing stuff") instead of insight ("clients don't know what we do after the pitch"). Push for sentences.

### Dot voting
**When:** narrowing anything: ideas, HMWs, clusters, options. The workhorse.
**Group and time:** whole room, 10 minutes.
**Materials:** dot stickers, 3-5 per person depending on option count.
**Run it:**
1. State the voting question precisely, written above the wall ("most worth testing in the next month", not "best").
2. Everyone votes at once, dots on stickies, stacking allowed or not (say which).
3. Count in the open, record the counts, and name the top 2-3. Seniors vote last or simultaneously, never first.
**Failure mode:** a mushy voting question. "Best idea" mixes feasibility, love, and politics; the question defines the criterion or the vote means nothing.

### Impact-effort 2x2
**When:** a shortlist needs a reality check before commitments get made.
**Group and time:** whole room or groups per shortlist chunk, 20 minutes.
**Materials:** a large 2x2 (impact vertical, effort horizontal), the shortlisted items on movable stickies.
**Run it:**
1. Calibrate the axes with one example the room agrees on, or every placement fight repeats the calibration fight.
2. Place items one at a time: whoever proposed it suggests a position, the room adjusts, 90 seconds per item.
3. Read the map: top-left (high impact, low effort) becomes actions; top-right becomes projects needing owners; bottom-right gets said goodbye to out loud.
**Failure mode:** everything drifts top-left because effort estimates come from enthusiasm. Ask "who is doing this and what do they stop doing" on every top-left placement.

### Now, next, later
**When:** sequencing what survived prioritization without the false precision of dates.
**Group and time:** whole room, 15 minutes.
**Materials:** three wall columns, the prioritized items.
**Run it:**
1. Define the horizons in this room's terms (now = this month, next = this quarter, later = parked with love).
2. Place items, with the rule that "now" must survive the capacity question: who actually has the hours?
3. Every "now" item leaves with an owner and a first step, captured for the close.
**Failure mode:** an eight-item "now" column. If everything is now, nothing is; force trades until now fits the real calendar.

## Decision

### Weighted decision matrix
**When:** choosing between 2-4 real options where the criteria can be named; turns a taste fight into a criteria conversation.
**Group and time:** whole room, 30 minutes.
**Materials:** a grid poster (options as columns, criteria as rows), dots or markers.
**Run it:**
1. Agree the criteria FIRST, before any option is discussed, 4-6 of them, weighted simply (double weight for the two that matter most).
2. Score each option per criterion, room by discussion or quick show of fingers, 2 minutes per cell region.
3. Do the math in the open. Then the crucial question: "the matrix says X, does that feel wrong to anyone?" A strong gut objection means a criterion is missing; find it and rerun, that's the exercise working.
**Failure mode:** reverse-engineering criteria to make the pre-favored option win. Locking criteria before discussing options is the whole defense; don't let new criteria in mid-scoring without naming what's happening.

### Fist to five
**When:** testing real agreement on a decision before it's declared made; ten times faster than "any objections?"
**Group and time:** whole room, 5-10 minutes.
**Materials:** hands.
**Run it:**
1. State the decision exactly as it would be written down.
2. Everyone votes simultaneously: 5 fingers (full yes) to fist (block).
3. Every 0, 1, and 2 speaks: "what would move you one finger up?" Address or adjust, revote once.
4. Record the decision with the final vote spread, not as fake unanimity.
**Failure mode:** running it after the boss has spoken. Fist to five before the senior voice weighs in, or it measures deference, not agreement.

## Closing

### Actions and owners round
**When:** the last 15 minutes of every workshop that decided anything.
**Group and time:** whole room, 15 minutes.
**Materials:** the day's walls, one visible actions list.
**Run it:**
1. Walk the wall: for each decision or "now" item, write the action, the named owner (a person, never a team), and the date.
2. Each owner says their action back in their own words, one sentence; if they rephrase it differently than intended, fix it now, not in the follow-up email.
3. Name what happens next per the intention brief's follow-up plan, and where the material lives.
**Failure mode:** owners assigned in absentia or by seniority reflex. An action nobody claimed out loud is a wish; leave it visibly unowned rather than fictionally owned.

### Plus / delta
**When:** the room's own 5-minute retro at the very end; also feeds workshop-retro.
**Group and time:** whole room, 5-10 minutes.
**Materials:** two flipchart columns.
**Run it:**
1. Plus: what should we keep doing in sessions like this? Popcorn, capture verbatim.
2. Delta: what would you change? Same.
3. Thank the room, point at one delta you'll act on next time, and close.
**Failure mode:** defending the design against deltas in the moment. Capture, thank, move on; the debrief is where it gets processed.
