# Job Scorecard

## When To Use
The brief is "two jobs in one title", and everyone means something different by "senior". Use it after the intake meeting and before anything is written for candidates. It answers: what must this person achieve in year one, and what evidence would show they can do it?

## When Not To Use
If the manager has not yet agreed what the job is, run Intake Meeting first; a scorecard built on a drifting brief only makes the drift look official. If you need the form interviewers fill in, that is Interview Scorecard, which uses these criteria.

## Inputs
- The signed-off intake summary, or your intake notes
- The manager's answers on two or three times the job went notably well and badly
- Any existing job description, for reference only
If you have none of this, I start from the role title and three main tasks and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
This is light job analysis as OPM describes it: list the tasks, then the competencies each task needs, and keep only criteria linked to a task. The evidence examples come from the critical incident technique (Flanagan, 1954, Psychological Bulletin): real moments when the work went well or badly, and what the person actually did. The role scorecard format is described generically. The failure it prevents: "senior" meaning years of service to one interviewer, scope to another and polish to a third, so three people assess three different jobs.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: the main tasks in order of time spent, two or three critical incidents (what happened, what the person did, the result), and who signs off the scorecard.
2. Tasks to competencies: for each task, the knowledge, skill or behaviour it needs. Any criterion with no task behind it is dropped.
3. First-year outcomes: 3 to 5 observable results, each with a measure as a [placeholder] the manager sets.
4. Criteria: keep 4 to 6. For each, a one-line definition, what strong evidence looks like (drawn from the incidents), must-have or trainable, and the stage where it is assessed (screen, interview, work sample, reference).
5. Wording pass: "culture fit", age-coded words and personality labels are replaced with the behaviour meant.
6. Split test: if the outcomes pull in two directions, show both sets side by side and ask the manager to choose one job or open two.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Job Scorecard: [Role title]
## Purpose
[One sentence: why this role exists]
## First-Year Outcomes
| Outcome | Measure | By when |
|---|---|---|
| [observable result] | [measure the manager sets] | [month] |
## Criteria
| Criterion | Definition | Strong evidence looks like | Must-have or trainable | Assessed at |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [criterion] | [one line] | [from a critical incident] | [must-have] | [stage] |
## Split Check
[One job, or the two competing sets of outcomes for the manager to choose between]
## Decision
[Hiring manager] approves the outcomes and criteria by [date]; [recruiter] uses them for every later step.
```

## Done When
- Every criterion links to a named task
- There are 4 to 6 criteria, each assessed at exactly one main stage
- Every outcome is observable, with a measure or a [placeholder]
- No word describes personality, age or "fit"

## Quality Bar
- The scorecard describes the job; it never rates, scores or compares a candidate.
- Strong evidence is written as what someone did, never as a trait.
- Trainable items stay off the list of must-haves and out of the screen.
- Measures are the manager's; I invent no targets or benchmarks.
- If two jobs hide in one title, the scorecard says so rather than blending them.
