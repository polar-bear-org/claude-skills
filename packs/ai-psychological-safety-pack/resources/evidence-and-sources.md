# Evidence and sources

Prepared 17 September 2026. These are original Polar Bear practice tools informed by the sources below. No source author or publisher endorses this pack. We have not tested whether using these skills improves psychological safety or workplace outcomes.

## The article behind this pack

Jayshree Seth and Amy C. Edmondson, [What Leaders Need to Know About AI and Psychological Safety](https://hbr.org/2026/09/what-leaders-need-to-know-about-ai-and-psychological-safety), Harvard Business Review, 17 September 2026.

The full article was reviewed in a user-supplied PDF. This pack includes original instructions and a paraphrased synthesis; it does not redistribute the article or its illustration.

The authors describe several ways AI may interact with speaking up: carrying an objection a person feels unable to raise directly, helping people articulate concerns across differences in language or status, providing a place to practise, and helping leaders generate challenges to their own plans. These are a framework and practitioner observations informed by research. They are not four experimentally established effects of using AI.

The authors explicitly leave open whether AI-assisted rehearsal builds psychological safety or becomes a substitute for it. Their leadership advice informs the pack's attention to real consultation, the response to disagreement, and visible follow-through. AI's apparent neutrality is a perception, not a property the pack assumes to be true.

Ordinary use of AI for editing or translation does not demonstrate that someone fears speaking up. An unattributed concern may warrant curiosity, but neither anonymity nor AI assistance proves the content correct or diagnoses a team's culture. The authors also note that workplace systems can retain information about who used them; the pack therefore does not promise anonymity.

The article cites other research and a business survey. We do not reproduce the survey percentage or treat those secondary references as independently verified findings here.

## Foundational research

Amy Edmondson, [Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams](https://dash.harvard.edu/entities/publication/13a7b031-0fdd-45ec-a7e0-2b80e2bc679f), Administrative Science Quarterly, 44(2), 350–383, 1999. DOI: 10.2307/2666999.

The Harvard repository abstract describes a field study of 51 teams in one manufacturing company. Team psychological safety was associated with learning behaviour. This supports taking the interpersonal conditions of learning seriously. It does not show that an AI prompt causes safety, that every agency will respond similarly, or that a short exercise can measure the construct.

## Polar Bear's design choices

The six workflows, output formats, templates, and fictional examples are our practical interpretation. They are not a validated assessment or an implementation prescribed by the source authors.

| Source idea | Practical design choice |
|---|---|
| People may use AI to help express a difficult concern | Preserve the person's evidence, uncertainty, language, and choice of channel |
| Leaders can use AI to examine objections | Label generated objections as hypotheses and take questions to people with relevant experience |
| AI-assisted preparation has uncertain effects on safety | Make rehearsal optional and avoid promises about another person's reaction |
| The response to a concern matters | Prepare acknowledgment, evidence checks, a reasoned decision, and follow-through |

## What a useful result looks like

A specific concern becomes clearer. A decision's assumptions become easier to test. A person has a choice about how to participate. A leader explains what they heard and what will happen next. These are observable outputs to review, not proof that a culture has changed.

Psychological safety is about the interpersonal consequences people expect when taking work-related risks such as asking for help or questioning a plan. It does not require agreement, relaxed standards, compulsory disclosure, or public participation.
