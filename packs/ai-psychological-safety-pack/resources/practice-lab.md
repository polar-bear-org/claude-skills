# Practice with a real work situation

The examples below are fictional. The suggested responses illustrate the pack's intended behaviour; they are not records of field testing or promises about outcomes.

## 1. A junior colleague questions a promotion process

**Situation:** A consultant has examples of mentoring work that are absent from the project summaries used for promotion. English is their second language. They want a clear way to ask how this work is considered.

**Use:** `concern-clarifier`, then `speak-up-rehearsal` if wanted.

**A useful opening:** I would like to understand how mentoring contributes to the promotion decision. My project summary covers delivery, but it does not include the onboarding sessions I ran. What evidence would help the reviewers consider that work?

**What the assistant should preserve:** The concern is about evidence and criteria, not a claim that the panel is biased. It should not invent a pattern of unfair decisions, insist the colleague speak publicly, or erase the concern to make the wording more deferential. If the colleague fears consequences, discuss a suitable route first.

## 2. An AI-written critique arrives without a name

**Situation:** A founder receives a document arguing that the proposed AI review process will reward polished writing rather than actual contribution. The founder asks who wrote it.

**Use:** `respond-to-ai-raised-concerns`.

**A useful response draft:** The concern about writing quality influencing our judgments deserves a check. I propose we examine whether our evidence guide separates contribution from presentation, then share what we find before the new process starts. Further examples can be raised through our existing feedback routes.

**What the assistant should preserve:** This is a proposal until the founder agrees to the check and timing. The assistant should assess the argument, not identify an author from logs or style. It should not infer that the whole team is afraid or claim that AI independently verified the critique.

## 3. The plan looks strong, but nobody has been asked

**Situation:** An agency wants to require an AI tool for all review self-assessments. Leadership expects time savings, but has not checked accessibility, data-handling rules, or whether people want another way to prepare.

**Use:** `ai-decision-stress-test`, followed by `invite-team-challenge`.

**A useful result:** A short list of unknowns, the roles who can answer them, and an invitation that makes clear whether the requirement can change. Potential objections are labelled as possibilities. People can provide written or private input without uploading their rehearsal chats.

**What the assistant should preserve:** It must not fabricate employee resistance. If the requirement is already fixed, the invitation must distinguish explanation from the implementation choices that remain open.

## 4. A concern was heard, but the decision stays

**Situation:** The agency will retain its promotion criteria this cycle. It has agreed to clarify acceptable evidence of mentoring. No owner or date has been set.

**Use:** `close-the-concern-loop`.

**A useful result:** An honest update distinguishing unchanged criteria from the planned evidence-guide improvement. The owner and timing remain unconfirmed. A route for further examples stays available.

**What the assistant should preserve:** It should not say everyone agreed or that the issue is resolved. A respectful decision can stand while disagreement remains.

## Try the pack with a colleague

Choose a fictional or low-sensitivity example. Run one skill, check whether the output preserves the concern and separates facts from hypotheses, then rewrite anything that does not sound like you. Practise a human conversation if useful. Afterwards, identify one concrete action and when you will return to it. No one needs to share a personal experience or a private AI chat.
