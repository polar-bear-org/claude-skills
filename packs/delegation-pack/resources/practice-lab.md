# Practice lab

All cases and dialogue below are fictional. These are expected-output examples for reviewing prompt quality, not demonstrations of measured effectiveness. Use aliases. Spend 15 minutes trying a prompt, 10 minutes comparing the result with the checks, and 5 minutes revising one real practice.

## Case 1 — A founder keeps rewriting client proposals

**Input:** A 35-person agency founder asks account lead Sam to prepare a renewal proposal due Friday. Sam has prepared two similar proposals. The founder wants to approve pricing and contractual promises but keeps rewriting the layout. Sam estimates six hours of work and has only three hours free unless a nonurgent internal report moves. The founder's initial view is “I need to review everything because the client matters.”

**Run:** delegation-capacity-check, then delegation-authority-map. Each can run independently with the supplied facts.

**Expected capacity note:** Six hours required versus three available leaves at least a three-hour gap before any coordination allowance. Move the internal report only if its owner approves, or narrow the proposal/date. Ask Sam to confirm the estimate. Do not recommend evenings as the default solution.

**Expected decision map:** Sam chooses structure and presentation within agreed client requirements. Sam recommends price; founder approves price and new contractual commitments. The founder owes a review window before the final deadline. Layout is not silently made a retained decision. Required client facts and past proposal access must be available.

**Expected first check:** Review a short outline and commercial assumptions before drafting the entire proposal. Agree what the founder will decide at that check. “Check every hour” fails the proportionality test.

**Useful closing draft:** “You own the proposal structure and wording within the agreed requirements. I will decide pricing and contract changes at our agreed review. We still need to confirm what moves to make room.”

**Checks:** Does the output preserve founder approvals while granting real discretion? Does it show the capacity gap? Does it avoid claiming Sam or the report owner has agreed? Can Sam challenge a late preference without being labelled resistant?

## Case 2 — The stretch task has no authority

**Input:** In an 80-person consultancy, Jo is asked to improve the resourcing spreadsheet across three departments. Jo can edit the file but cannot get reliable availability data. Department leads keep changing priorities. The manager says “Bring me solutions, not problems.” There is no urgent safety risk, but the project is slipping. Jo has already proposed a shared data deadline; no lead has accepted it.

**Run:** delegation-coach-without-taking-over or delegation-reset-handoff.

**Expected diagnosis:** Cross-department authority and priority alignment are missing. This is not enough evidence of a capability or motivation problem. More option generation alone cannot resolve the manager-owned blocker.

**Expected support split:** The manager convenes or contacts the authorized department decision makers to agree a data owner and decision route. Jo proposes a minimum viable data format and documents missing fields. Both actions get dates. Deadline changes remain provisional until the relevant sponsor decides.

**Expected recovery option:** Pilot with one willing department if that still serves a useful purpose and the sponsor accepts reduced scope; otherwise pause the cross-department promise until authority is established. Do not tell Jo to enforce compliance without authority.

**Expected next check:** Review whether owners and response dates are agreed, then inspect a sample dataset. Avoid rating Jo's confidence or imposing daily reporting as a cure for inaccessible data.

**Checks:** Does the manager retain their own blocker? Is support explicit without automatically taking back all execution? Does the output preserve uncertainty about why leads have not supplied data? Does it contain a feasible recovery path?

## Case 3 — An urgent client-release problem

**Input:** A junior producer owns routine newsletter preparation. Ten minutes before a scheduled send, they discover what may be another client's confidential attachment in the draft. They ask the manager to take a look. The manager is tempted to insist on three options to prevent “reverse delegation.” The producer has access to the draft but the scenario does not establish permission to change the sending schedule or determine whether an incident occurred.

**Run:** delegation-coach-without-taking-over.

**Expected immediate response:** Treat potential irreversible disclosure as urgent. Involve the authorized person who can pause or prevent release and the appropriate internal incident route. Do not delay this while demanding options. Do not have the AI click send, access client data, or decide disclosure obligations.

**Expected ownership note:** The producer supplies the minimum necessary facts through approved channels. The authorized release owner decides containment. Record a temporary transfer of that decision if needed, the reason, and a later review of routine ownership. After containment, investigate the workflow and clarify future stop/escalation authority.

**Expected dialogue draft:** “Thank you for raising this now. Let's get the authorized release owner to prevent an unintended send and check the facts. We'll clarify routine ownership after the immediate issue is contained.”

**Checks:** Immediate help precedes coaching. The output does not assert a breach occurred, invent authority, or punish speaking up. It separates routine delegation from incident decisions and gives a clear human next step.

## Review your real handoff

After one task, compare the agreed outcome with delivery evidence. Ask both people how much preparation, support, and avoidable rework the handoff required. Ask whether authority and workload matched responsibility. Choose one change for the next handoff and record an owner and review point. A better second task is useful local feedback; it does not establish that the pack caused the improvement.
