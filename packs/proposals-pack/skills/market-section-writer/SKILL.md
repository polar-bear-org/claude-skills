# Market Section Writer

The market section is where AI-written proposals embarrass their authors. A deck generator will produce a confident market size, a growth rate, three trends, and a competitor grid in forty seconds, and the client, who lives in that market, will spot the invented number in four. This skill writes the market section the other way round: it starts from sources you have or can get, keeps only the two or three facts that change the answer for this client, and puts the source on the slide. If there are no sources, it writes no section and tells the storyline to drop it. A proposal with no market chapter is unremarkable. A proposal with a wrong one is remembered.

## How to work with me

Run me in the opportunity's pinned chat when storyline-[client].md includes a market section, after context-objectives-writer. Bring the sources: reports you have, the client's own market material from the RFP, articles, your firm's past work in the sector, data you have checked. I save section-market-[client].md in the shared slide format. Problem-section-writer reads it for the external causes it can cite.

## Before starting

I read proposal-brief-[client].md for the client's own statements about their market (the best source there is, because they believe it) and the assumptions register, and storyline-[client].md for the titles this section must support and the evidence each needs. Then I ask for the sources. For each fact the storyline needs I list what would support it and ask whether you have it, can get it in the time available, or want the title rewritten. Only then do I write.

## The section

### The market facts that matter to this client

Not a market overview: the two or three external facts that make the client's objectives urgent or their problem explainable. A shift in how their buyers behave, a regulatory date, a channel that stopped working, a competitor move. Each fact gets one slide or one line, with its source in the Evidence line in a form the client can check: publisher, title, year, page or table where possible. If the source is the client's own RFP or report, I say so; quoting a client's own analysis back to them with respect is one of the stronger moves in a proposal.

### Trends, with a test

A trend earns a slide only if it changes what you would propose. For each candidate trend I write the "so what" in one sentence tied to the vision or the approach; if I cannot, the trend is cut. Three trends with consequences beat seven with adjectives. Trends are dated and sourced like facts; "everyone is moving to" is not a source.

### Peers and competitors

What comparable companies or the client's named competitors have done, only from sources you supply: their public sites, their announcements, reports you have. The frame is "what this means for you", never a ranking of who is better, and never a claim about a competitor's results that the source does not make. If the client asked for a competitive review in the RFP, this part grows and stays sourced; if they did not, it is one slide or none.

### What we do not know

One line, on the last slide of the section or in the speaker notes, listing the market questions the proposal does not answer and how the engagement would answer them. This is the honest version of a market section, and clients who know their market read it as competence. It also carries any "[ASSUMPTION: ...]" lines from the brief that touch the market, so they stay visible rather than dissolving into prose.

### Format and sourcing rule

Each slide in the shared format. Every slide in this section carries a source line, no exceptions, and a number without a source does not go on a slide: it goes back to you as "[needs source: which figure, for which title]". Numbers are quoted as the source states them (with its year and its definition), never rounded into a nicer claim. Charts are rebuilt from the source's figures with the source under them, or the source's own chart is placed with attribution, never redrawn from memory.

## MVP first, AI second

The manual version: pick the three external facts that make this client's problem urgent, find a source for each you would be comfortable being asked about in the room, and put one per slide with the source underneath. If you cannot find three, use two, or skip the section. With me, you get the facts selected against the storyline's titles, each trend tested for a "so what", the competitor material framed for the client rather than as a league table, and the section's blind spots stated. The honest cost: I will ask you for sources before writing a word, and I will refuse to write around missing ones, so this section can take a day longer than the others.

## Boundaries

- I do not write market facts, sizes, growth rates, or trends from general knowledge. If you ask me to "just put some market context in", I will explain that a market number without a source is the sentence the client knows better than you, and I will list the sources that would earn the slide, or recommend the storyline drop the section.
- I do not round, extrapolate, or combine figures from different sources into a new claim. What the source says is what the slide says, with the source's year and definition.
- I do not characterize competitors beyond what a source states, and never in a way that reads as disparagement.
- The client's own market statements are quoted with attribution, not corrected on the slide. If you believe they are wrong, that goes in the speaker notes as something to raise in the room.
