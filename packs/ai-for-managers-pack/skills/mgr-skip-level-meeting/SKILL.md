# Skip-Level Meeting

## When To Use
You run skip-levels with your reports' reports, or you are preparing for one with your boss's boss. Either way, the question is the same: how do I make this meeting useful to everyone without it going around the manager in the middle?

## When Not To Use
If you want one person's reasons to stay, run Stay Interview; a group meeting across levels will not surface them. If you need leadership to decide something specific, write a Managing Up Brief first and bring it.

## Inputs
- Which mode: you run it (with your reports' reports) or you attend it (with your manager's manager).
- The group or person, the time available, and anything already on the table.
- For running one: notes from past skip-levels, if any.
If you have none of this, I start from the mode and the length of the meeting, and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
The skip-level meeting is common management practice: a leader meets people two levels away to hear how the work really goes. Its value is listening across a gap; its risk is going around the middle manager, who then learns about the complaints from the grapevine. This skill keeps the middle manager informed, keeps the meeting on the work, and turns what was heard into themes nobody can trace back to one person.

## Workflow
1. Ask up to three questions: which mode, what you most want to learn or say, and how many mentions a theme needs before you report it (you set this number).
2. The middle manager first. Running one: tell your direct report before the invite goes out, say why, and agree what you will share back. Attending one: tell your own manager you have been asked and what you plan to raise.
3. Agenda, listening-heavy. Running: a short opening on why you are there and what happens to what you hear, then questions, then what you will do. Attending: what you want them to know, what you want to ask, and one thing you need.
4. Question set about the work: what gets in the way, what they would change, what leadership should know and does not. Nothing that asks people to assess their manager or a colleague.
5. Themes without names. Group what you heard, drop any detail that identifies a person (role, project, a phrase only one person uses), and keep a theme only if it reaches your threshold.
6. Follow-up: what you will do, what you will tell the group, and the date. Share the themes with the middle manager before or with the group.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Skip-Level Meeting Plan
**Mode:** [running / attending] **With:** [group or role] **Date:** [date] **Length:** [time]
**Middle manager told:** [name], on [date], agreed to share back [what]
## Agenda
| Part | Purpose | Time |
|---|---|---|
| [opening] | [why we meet, what happens to what is said] | [time] |
| [questions] | [listen] | [time] |
## Questions
- [question about the work]
## Themes heard
| Theme | Mentions | What it asks of leadership |
|---|---|---|
| [theme, no identifying detail] | [count, at or above threshold] | [ask] |
## Decision
[Your name] decides which themes to act on and tells [group] and [middle manager] by [date].
```

## Done When
- The middle manager is named in the plan with the date they were told.
- No question invites a verdict on a named person.
- Every theme meets the threshold and carries no identifying detail.
- The follow-up has a date and says who hears what.

## Quality Bar
- Listening time outweighs talking time in the agenda.
- Nothing said in the room becomes an evaluation of the middle manager.
- Anything that sounds like a grievance or a safety concern goes to HR, not into a theme.
- Promise only what you can deliver, with a date.
- Themes, never names: Claude never attributes a comment to a person.
