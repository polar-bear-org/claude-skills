# Win/Loss Review

## When To Use
You lost a pitch and the only feedback was "we went another way". Or you won and cannot say why. Use this within a few weeks of a decision, while you still remember, to answer: what did I expect, what happened, and what do I keep or change next time?

## When Not To Use
If the deal is still open, run Pipeline Review. If the client has just raised a concern and you want to answer it now, run Objection Response.

## Inputs
- For each decided deal: what you proposed, the dates, the outcome and anything the client said
- Your notes, the proposal and the email thread
- Whether you can ask the client for a short conversation
If you have none of this, I start from what you remember of one deal and mark every client reason as "our view, not confirmed".

## Approach
The review follows the four questions of the After Action Review, from USAID's 2006 technical guidance, built on US Army practice: what was expected to happen, what actually happened, what went well and why, what can be improved and how. The guidance is clear that a review is not a critique or a complaint session, and that matters here: a lost deal invites blaming the buyer or a colleague, and blame teaches nothing. The client's reasons are the part most often guessed; a guess written as fact will steer every next proposal wrong.

## Workflow
1. Ask at most three questions: which deals to review, what the client said when they decided, and whether you can ask them for a short conversation.
2. For each deal, answer the first two questions in order. Expected: the outcome, the timing and why you believed it then. Actual: what happened, with dates. Keep them separate; writing the lesson before the facts is the commonest slip.
3. Work out why the difference, as a sequence of decisions: who you spoke to, what you learned in discovery, how the proposal was framed, how follow-up went. Decisions and sequence only, never the client's or a colleague's character.
4. The client's side: draft 3 to 5 short, open questions and a request that makes no easy to say. You ask, only with the client's agreement. Without an answer, mark each client reason "our view, not confirmed".
5. Keep and change: at most three of each, each tied to a habit or a skill you will use next time (for example, a firmer discovery step, a walk-through before the proposal goes out).
6. Patterns: look across deals only when there are several. With one or two, write "no pattern yet" and leave it.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Win/Loss Review
Period: [dates] · Deals reviewed: [count]
## [Deal] · [won / lost] · [decision date]
| Expected | What happened | Why the difference |
|---|---|---|
| [what we expected] | [what happened] | [decision or step] |
What went well and why: [answer]
What to improve and how: [answer]
Client's view: [their words, confirmed] or [our view, not confirmed]
## Questions for the client (asked with their agreement)
1. [open question]
## Keep (at most three)
- [habit] · next used in [skill or step]
## Change (at most three)
- [habit] · next used in [skill or step]
## Patterns across deals
[pattern, or "no pattern yet"]
## Decision
[Your name] decides by [date] whether to ask the client, and which one change to try on the next proposal.
```

## Done When
- Each deal has expected and actual written separately, with dates
- Every client reason is confirmed in their words or marked as your view
- Keep and change hold three items each at most
- Patterns appear only when there are enough deals to show one

## Quality Bar
- No assessment of the buyer or of anyone on your team
- Feedback is asked for only with the client's agreement, and a no is respected
- Lessons are tied to something you will do differently, not a general principle
- The client's reasons are theirs to give; anything not confirmed is marked as your view.
