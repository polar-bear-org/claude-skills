# Value Discovery Questions

## When To Use
You want to price on value and do not know what the work is worth to this client. Run it before the discovery call, so you walk in with a short set of honest questions, and again after it, to sort what the client actually said from what you assumed.

## When Not To Use
If the client has already told you the outcome, its worth and their alternative, go straight to the Value-Based Pricing Worksheet. If the call ahead is about the price itself, use the Price Conversation Prep Sheet instead.

## Inputs
- What you know about the client and the work so far: their message, the brief, your notes from earlier calls.
- Who will be on the call, by role, and how long you have.
- After the call: your notes, with the client's answers as they gave them.
If you have none of this, I start from the six question groups below and mark the set as a first draft.

## Approach
Economic value to the customer, from Forbis and Mehta (Business Horizons, 1981) and summarised by Utpal Dholakia in Harvard Business Review (2016): what the work is worth equals the cost of the client's next-best alternative plus the value that differs between you and it. You cannot compute that without the client's own numbers, so this skill only asks. The failure it prevents: a confident "value price" built on a figure you imagined during the drive to the meeting, which falls apart the first time the client asks where it came from. Today the next-best alternative is often the client's own AI, and the questions ask about it plainly.

## Workflow
1. I ask up to three questions: what the client has asked for so far, in their words; how long the call is; and what you already know about who decides.
2. I draft questions in six groups, in this order, because each one rests on the one before: the outcome and how the client would measure it; what it is worth in their numbers (revenue, cost, risk, time); the cost of doing nothing or of waiting; the next-best alternative (another provider, doing it in house, their own AI) and what that costs them; what differs between you and that alternative, good and bad; who decides and how.
3. Every question is open. I strip out leading and loaded ones ("wouldn't you agree this costs you a lot?") and anything that pushes the client toward a number. A question about their own AI is asked with curiosity, never as a trap.
4. You pick a short set that fits the call, usually one or two per group. A list of twenty questions turns a conversation into an interrogation.
5. I lay out the answer grid with a "client's words" column left blank. I never fill an answer, a number or a quote, not even as a guess.
6. After the call you paste your notes. You mark each value driver "stated by client", "inferred" or "unknown"; I sort the grid and list the gaps worth one follow-up email.

## Done When
- Six groups are covered, in order, and every question is open.
- The "client's words" column is blank or filled only from your notes.
- Each value driver carries a status you set.

## Quality Bar
- No question suggests a number or a conclusion to the client.
- The client's own AI is treated as a fair alternative, never mocked.
- Notes record what was said, never a judgment of the person who said it.
- Answers are the client's own words; Claude never fills them in.
