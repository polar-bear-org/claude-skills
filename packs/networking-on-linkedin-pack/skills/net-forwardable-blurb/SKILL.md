# Forwardable Intro Blurb

## When To Use
Someone agreed to introduce you and the introduction has not happened, because they have to write it. Use this right after the yes, so the introducer gets a short note they can forward as it is. It answers: what does the other person need to read to say yes or no in one minute?

## When Not To Use
If nobody has agreed to introduce you yet, run the Introduction Ask first. If you are the one introducing two other people, run the Double Opt-In Introduction. If the introducer knows you both well and prefers to write it themselves, let them.

## Inputs
- Your Elevator Pitch line
- What you know about the person to be introduced: their Person Research Brief or public links
- The one conversation you would ask for (topic, length) and the length limit you want for the blurb
If you have none of this, I start from your elevator pitch and the topic of the conversation, and mark the output as a first draft.

## Approach
The forwardable note, as summarised from Harvard Business Review ("How to ask for an email introduction", Ruchika T. Malhotra, 2019): the introducer lends you their network, so include a note they can forward and do not make them write one. The judgment is in brevity and in asking for little. The failure it prevents: a kind yes that sits in someone's inbox for weeks because the introduction needs twenty minutes of their writing.

## Workflow
1. Ask three questions: who is introducing you to whom, what one conversation you want, and the length limit for the blurb (you set it).
2. Give 2 or 3 points for "who you are", taken from your elevator pitch line. No adjectives about yourself.
3. Give 2 or 3 points for "why this person specifically", each resting on a sourced public fact about their work, with link and date. Nothing private and nothing guessed.
4. Give 2 or 3 points for "what you would ask": one conversation, its topic, its length. One ask only.
5. Give the points for "how easy it is to decline": a clear way out that needs no explanation.
6. You write the blurb from the points. Then I check it: within your length limit; no claim the introducer would have to vouch for and cannot; no attachment needed; forwardable without a single edit.
7. Remind you of the order: the blurb goes to the introducer, who asks the other side first. The introduction happens only if they say yes.

## Output Format
```markdown
# Forwardable Intro Blurb
**Introducer:** [name] · **To be introduced to:** [name] · **Length limit (you set):** [ ]
## Points to write from
| Part | Points | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Who you are | [2 or 3 points from your pitch line] | your pitch |
| Why this person | [2 or 3 points] | [link, date] |
| What you would ask | [one conversation, topic, length] | your brief |
| Easy to decline | [the way out] | n/a |
## Your blurb
[your words, written from the points]
## Checks on your blurb
| Check | Pass or flag | Line |
|---|---|---|
| Within length | [ ] | [ ] |
| Nothing the introducer must vouch for | [ ] | [ ] |
| Forwardable without edits | [ ] | [ ] |
## Decision
You write the blurb and send it to [introducer] by [date]; they decide whether and how to forward it.
```

## Done When
- All four parts have points, and every fact about the other person has a source
- The blurb itself is in your words, not assembled by Claude
- The checks are run on what you wrote, with flags tied to lines
- The double opt-in order is stated

## Quality Bar
- One ask, for one conversation, with its length
- Facts about the person to be introduced come from public sources only
- No line puts the introducer's reputation behind a claim they cannot check
- Short beats complete: if a part does not fit the limit, it goes
- You write the blurb; the introducer decides whether and how to forward it, and you send it yourself.
