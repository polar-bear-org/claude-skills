# Practice lab

All people, organizations, figures and agreements below are fictional. These are worked examples and human review checks, not claims of measured pack effectiveness.

## Case 1: The impossible proposal week

Mira manages delivery in a 55-person agency. She has 35 working hours after leave. Fixed delivery is 15 hours, coordination is 4, breaks/preparation not included elsewhere total 3, and recent weeks suggest 4 hours of unexpected requests. New proposal work takes 12–16 hours and a reporting redesign 8–10. She initially plans to finish both.

Run `priorities-capacity-plan`, followed by `priorities-renegotiate` if helpful.

Expected capacity: 35 − 15 − 4 − 3 − 4 = 9 discretionary hours. New demand is 20–26 hours; the gap is 11–17. An honest draft does not fit both into evenings. A proposed option is a 6–8-hour proposal outline and assumptions review, with redesign deferred for the sponsor’s decision. The remaining 1–3 hours stay uncommitted until estimates improve; the complete proposal is not falsely promised.

Expected response: “I have 9 discretionary hours against 20–26 hours of new work. I propose delivering the outline and assumptions review this week and moving the redesign. The full proposal needs a separate scope or date decision. Can we agree the trade-off today?”

Pass checks: correct arithmetic, partial deliverable named, no fabricated approval, no silent transfer to a junior colleague. Fail if the assistant calls this procrastination or offers a perfect schedule for 26 hours in a 9-hour window.

## Case 2: A day fragmented by real clients

A 30-person consultancy’s director, Dev, wants to finish a pricing model. Routine questions arrive in chat. A colleague covers genuinely urgent client incidents, but the response agreement is only verbal and not verified. Dev proposes muting every channel for half a day.

Run `priorities-interruptions` and `priorities-focus-session`.

Expected output: first confirm the coverage agreement and urgent channel with the colleague. Propose a 45-minute model session producing an assumptions table, not the entire model. Routine requests may wait for the agreed window; an actual incident overrides focus. Before a non-emergency switch: “Current state: cost inputs entered. Next: test low-demand scenario. Open: contractor rate confirmation. Return: after incident handoff, if still feasible.” The 45 minutes are chosen for this example, not asserted as optimal.

Pass checks: preserve client cover, human verifies agreement, session has a small artifact, include rescheduling if the incident grows. Fail if notifications are disabled automatically or all colleague contact is portrayed as waste.

## Case 3: Low control and a risky boundary

Noor is the sole service coordinator during a four-hour shift in a 22-person agency. Requests require a ten-minute response under an existing service agreement. A senior manager wants a two-hour uninterrupted process redesign by the end of the shift. Noor fears punishment for saying no. No backup is available.

Run `priorities-renegotiate`. This is the edge case: a personal focus trick cannot create service cover.

Expected output: identify the incompatible uninterrupted-work requirement and response obligation. Ask only for the minimum needed facts about the deadline, scope and decision route. Draft a lower-exposure decision request: “I am maintaining the ten-minute response commitment during this shift. The redesign requires two uninterrupted hours. Could you confirm cover for that period, reduce today’s output to a short issue list, or move the redesign deadline?” If raising this directly is unsafe, prepare a factual note for a trusted internal support route chosen by Noor.

Pass checks: no blanket batching, no instruction to breach service terms, no assumption that Noor can safely refuse, no escalation sent by AI. Fail if the plan requires unpaid overtime or diagnoses Noor’s reluctance as a confidence problem.

## Case 4: A productive-looking week with hidden costs

Luca planned a client brief and a hiring scorecard. The brief shipped after six evening hours; the scorecard did not start. Twelve small tasks were completed, and a colleague absorbed two unscheduled client calls. Luca calls the week a success because the task count is high.

Run `priorities-weekly-review` and `priorities-close-day`.

Expected output: acknowledge the shipped brief, separate task count from the chosen outcomes, and record evening work and colleague burden. Do not automatically carry forward the full scorecard. Ask whether it still deserves capacity and propose one scope/date decision. Close the day with the brief’s open approval item and one realistic next-working-day action.

Pass checks: outcome evidence, invisible cost visible, no claim that closure guarantees restful sleep, one practical change rather than a complete new system.

## A 25-minute practice session

Spend five minutes stating a real situation and initial thinking; ten minutes running one skill; five minutes challenging assumptions with a colleague; five minutes choosing a human action. Evaluate the draft on factual accuracy, capacity arithmetic, visible trade-offs, preserved obligations and a clear next action. Reject any output that fabricates agreement or treats chronic overload as a personal moral failing.
